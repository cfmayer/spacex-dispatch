The Morning Picture

USSF-627 flies; the double catch is a day away

A Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base at 11:36 p.m. EST Wednesday with the classified USSF-627 mission for the U.S. Space Force, two days after its first target date, and its booster landed on a droneship in the Pacific, SpaceX posted. At Starbase, the 26 Starlink satellites for Starship Flight 15 were rolled out to Ship 42, and airspace closures went up for Friday's 5:45 p.m. EST launch and Saturday's planned ship catch.

The Space Development Agency (SDA) is paying SpaceX $9.9 million to study its Starlink laser terminal for missile-tracking satellites, and Ookla found that Starlink is winning city customers faster than rural ones. SPCX fell 2.33% as Wall Street rallied, on the day a secondhand report said Anthropic would not renew its compute contract with SpaceX.

I · Human Spaceflight & Launches

USSF-627 launches on its third date

A night launch from California for the Space Force, while Starbase loads Ship 42 and closes the skies for Friday's flight.

Falcon 9 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 4E (SLC-4E) at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California at 11:36 p.m. EST Wednesday, Spaceflight Now reported, two hours into a window that opened at 9:35 p.m. The target moved to 11:36 p.m. on Wednesday evening, Launch Library 2 shows; no reason was given. "All systems are looking good, and weather is favorable for liftoff," SpaceX posted at 10:47 p.m. EST. The mission had first been set for Monday night, KSBY reported, and then for Tuesday.

The first stage, booster 1103, landed on the droneship Of Course I Still Love You about eight and a half minutes after liftoff on its seventh flight, after NROL-179, two SDA missions and three Starlink missions, Space.com reported. The payload is classified, and SpaceX ended its webcast after the landing. It was the second Space Force launch from Vandenberg in six days after USSF-432 on Nov. 6, the 120th Falcon 9 launch of 2026 and the rocket's 15th national-security flight of the year, the outlet said.

Flight 15: satellites loaded, skies closed

"The Starlink Speed Loader has left Starfactory to load Ship 42 with 26 Starlink V3s," NASASpaceflight posted at 5:14 p.m. EST Wednesday. Temporary flight restrictions for Friday's launch and for the potential catch of Ship 42 on Saturday were published, SpaceNotices reported at 11:57 a.m. EST, and the first notice to airmen for the ship's reentry over Mexico followed at 8:19 p.m. EST.

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) operations advisory on Thursday listed a launch window of 5:45 to 7:40 p.m. EST Friday and a ship reentry window of 3:56 to 7:14 p.m. EST Saturday, each with a backup a day later. SpaceX's timeline has Booster 22 caught 6 minutes 35 seconds after liftoff, the satellites deployed between 35 and 78 minutes into flight, and the ship caught 22 hours 32 minutes after liftoff, or about 4:18 p.m. EST Saturday after an on-time launch. The ship will try to return only if the systems needed for reentry, landing and the tower approach are healthy, SpaceX said. The flight is still pending regulatory approval.

Starship status

Starship reached orbit for the first time on Flight 14, launched Sept. 28, Space.com has reported. Flight 15, from Starbase Pad 2, is the first catch attempt for a Super Heavy V3 booster; three earlier boosters were caught, and two of them flew again, SpaceX says. A 5,000-foot "high energy ground testing" restriction for SpaceX's Massey's test site starting Nov. 19 has been published, SpaceNotices noted Thursday at 7:27 a.m. EST; such restrictions "are usually posted for ship static fire or booster cryo testing." Flight 16 is expected to be the first Starship launch from Launch Complex 39A (LC-39A) in Florida, NASASpaceflight has reported.

USSF-317 holds for Saturday night

SpaceX still targets Saturday, Nov. 14, at 10:55 p.m. EST for USSF-317 from SLC-4E, with a four-hour window and a backup on Sunday at 10:41 p.m. EST, its mission page shows. The booster makes its 11th flight after NROL-105, USSF-385 and eight Starlink missions and is to land on Of Course I Still Love You; residents of Santa Barbara, San Luis Obispo and Ventura counties may hear sonic booms. The FAA advisory now lists Saturday night as the primary window, 10:55 p.m. to 3:38 a.m. EST.

III · Starlink

Starlink moves to the city

Ookla's speed-test data show urban users growing more than twice as fast as rural ones, and upload speeds up 42%.

The number of Starlink users running Speedtest in urban areas of the US grew 73% year over year in the first half of 2026, more than double the 33% growth in rural areas, Ookla said in a report published Wednesday, Fierce Network reported. Ookla matched its speed tests to U.S. Census Bureau data to sort users, Broadband Breakfast said. "Urban users grew faster than rural users in every state but Alaska and Hawaii in the first half of 2026," said Sue Marek, Ookla's editorial director. "Many people think Starlink is only for rural users - but we are showing that isn't the case."

Median upload speeds rose 42%, from 14.63 to 20.73 megabits per second, and improved in all 50 states and Washington, D.C., which Marek tied to SpaceX's V2 Mini satellites; about 7,550 had been launched by early October, per astronomer Jonathan McDowell's tracker. The number of states with latency under 40 milliseconds more than doubled. About nine in 10 new users switched from cable, DSL or fixed wireless, helped by promotional prices of $29 to $39 a month in some regions. Starlink's median download speed still trails all three major US 5G fixed-wireless providers, Ookla found, in part because its satellites concentrate coverage over the Northern Hemisphere's middle latitudes.

The subscriber base

SpaceX said on its third-quarter call in late October that Starlink added 1.6 million broadband consumer subscribers in the quarter, for 13.6 million in 174 markets; Ookla's data put about 22% of Starlink customers in the US, its largest market, Fierce Network noted. On that call, Musk said a single Starship launch, about half loaded, had added the bandwidth of 10 Falcon 9 launches.

IV · Defense

Starlink lasers, studied for missile tracking

The Space Development Agency pays SpaceX $9.9 million to test whether its Plaser terminal can serve missile-warning satellites.

SpaceX won a $9,921,992 contract, with options worth up to $12,666,379, "for a risk reduction study on their Plug and Play Laser, also known as Plaser," covering the study, "the delivery of representative hardware, and support to missile warning and tracking system integration," according to the Department of War's Nov. 10 contract announcement, reproduced by AwardTape (contract FA2401-27-C-0001). The SDA awarded it Nov. 9 under an open broad agency announcement; the work is in Hawthorne, California, and Redmond, Washington, and is to finish by Oct. 7, 2027.

The study comes as the Space Force works to connect satellite networks built around different optical standards, SpaceNews reported. SpaceX is building the Space Data Network Backbone, a relay constellation formerly called MILNET, under a $2.29 billion agreement from May, and that network relies on Plaser terminals; an SDA official has said the agency's own terminals, built to its optical standard, cannot exchange data directly with Plaser, Defense WarRoom reported. The announcement does not say which satellites the hardware is meant for.

V · AI & Compute

Musk shrugs off an Anthropic report

Secondhand analyst notes said SpaceX's biggest compute customer won't renew; Musk said SpaceX would take the capacity back.

Anthropic told sell-side analysts that its compute contract with SpaceX "will not be extended beyond the middle of next year," according to meeting notes posted on X on Wednesday by Jukan, a semiconductor analyst who said he did not attend the meeting, DailyMech reported. Neither company has confirmed the remark. Anthropic's amended IPO filing of Nov. 10 says it agreed to spend "up to $120.5 billion over terms extending into 2029," about $3.1 billion a month once all capacity is delivered, expected in December, and that either side may cancel on 90 days' notice after non-cancelable periods ending in January and March 2027.

A compute customer labeled "Customer B" made up about 34.5% of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue, and a news report has identified it as Anthropic, the outlet noted. "We would be happy to take the compute back. No problem. But I think the opposite is going to happen," Elon Musk wrote at 12:40 p.m. EST. SPCX closed down 2.33% while the Nasdaq 100 rose 2.28% (see Section VII).

Musk's power math

Replying to a post about SpaceX's order of solar-cell equipment for 100 gigawatts (GW) a year, Musk wrote at 2:05 p.m. EST: "Actually, we’re aiming for 1TW/year from Earth, rising to over 1PW/year from the Moon." He added at 3:22 p.m. that "100GW in space is roughly equal to 800GW on Earth," because ground solar needs batteries at night and backup power in winter. On data centers, he said SpaceX "has a chance of going about half the GW next year of actually usable compute," since "it takes us ~2 months working 24/7 to get a large compute cluster working reliably. I’d guess closer to 6 months for others."

Southaven permit upheld

Mississippi's Environmental Quality Permit Board denied environmental groups' appeal of the air permit for 41 gas turbines at the Southaven plant that helps power SpaceX's Colossus 2 data center in Memphis, finding "insufficient evidence" after a two-day hearing, Action News 5 reported. The Southern Environmental Law Center called the permit "deeply flawed." Musk replied to a post on the ruling: "Much appreciated, Mississippi."
