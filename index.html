The Morning Picture

Flight 15 is stacked; the license is not yet in hand

Ship 42 was rolled to Starbase Pad 2 on Thursday and stacked on Booster 22 for today's 5:45 p.m. EST launch, the first attempt to catch both stages of an orbital rocket. SpaceX's flight page still said the launch was "pending regulatory approval" on Friday morning, and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) had not published an updated launch license when NASASpaceflight went to press. In California, USSF-317 slipped three days to Tuesday night.

SpaceX laid out more than $74 billion in planned supercompute campuses in Texas and Arizona, Bloomberg reported that the SpaceX–Tesla merger talks are progressing, and Ukraine's president said Starshield speeds are being limited again, which SpaceX called false. SPCX closed up 0.14% at $139.94.

I · Human Spaceflight & Launches

Starship Flight 15 counts down to a double catch

Booster 22 and Ship 42 are stacked at Pad 2 for a 75-minute window that opens at 5:45 p.m. EST, with the FAA license modification still outstanding.

SpaceX rolled Ship 42 from the production site to Pad 2 on Thursday afternoon, Spaceflight Now posted at 2:14 p.m. EST, and began hoisting it onto Booster 22 at 5:21 p.m., the outlet said. "Ship 42 moves to the Starbase pad ahead of launch," SpaceX posted at 7:56 p.m. EST, and "Countdown underway at Starbase for a potentially historic Starship mission," NASASpaceflight posted at 11:31 p.m.

The window opens at 5:45 p.m. EST and runs 75 minutes, SpaceX says; the flight still awaited regulatory approval on Friday morning. At the time of writing, "the FAA has not yet published an updated launch license for the flight," NASASpaceflight reported; navigational warnings show backup opportunities from Nov. 14 to Nov. 20 in the same time slot.

The catch plan

Booster 22 is to fly a boostback burn of about 41 seconds and be caught at about T+6 minutes 35 seconds after a landing burn that steps down from 13 engines to five, then three, NASASpaceflight explained. Ship 42 is to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites, coast for most of a day past three checkpoints where SpaceX can end the mission early (north of Hawaii, off Chile, or in the North Pacific), and return over Mexico and South Texas for a catch about 22 hours 32 minutes after liftoff, or about 4:18 p.m. EST Saturday after an on-time launch. Road closures run from noon to 12:30 a.m. EST on both Friday and Saturday. Both Starship launch notices to airmen for Mexican airspace were posted Thursday, and "the ascent debris area is significantly larger than it was for Flight 14," SpaceNotices noted at 11:15 a.m. EST; the last missing notices for the ship catch followed at 12:55 p.m.

Starship status

Flight 14, launched Sept. 28, was Starship's first orbital mission: Booster 21 lost two Raptor engines but made a soft splashdown in the Gulf, and Ship 41 deployed all 26 Starlink V3 satellites before a helium leak and an engine loss led SpaceX to bring it down early in a North Pacific contingency zone, NASASpaceflight recounted. Flight 15 flies from Starbase Pad 2 and is the first catch attempt for a Super Heavy V3 booster; three earlier boosters were caught, SpaceX says. Flight 16 is expected to be the first Starship launch from Launch Complex 39A (LC-39A) in Florida, NASASpaceflight has reported.

USSF-317 slips to Tuesday

SpaceX now targets Tuesday, Nov. 17, for USSF-317 from Space Launch Complex 4E (SLC-4E) at Vandenberg Space Force Base, with a four-hour window opening at 10:14 p.m. EST and a backup on Wednesday at 11:54 p.m. EST, its mission page shows. The Space Force mission had been set for Saturday, Nov. 14, at 10:55 p.m. EST, after earlier targets of Thursday and Friday; no reason was given. The FAA's operations advisory on Friday still listed the superseded Saturday window.

Quick Hit

AST SpaceMobile held an event Thursday in New York on Blacklift, a three-stage reusable rocket meant to carry up to five of its BlueBird satellites per launch, with a first flight planned for late 2028. "We cannot get enough launches on time," chief executive Abel Avellan said (SpaceNews).

III · Starlink

Ukraine says Starshield is throttled again; SpaceX says no

Zelenskyy said a 100-mph limit is back over occupied territory and the front line, hampering drone interceptors; SpaceX said nothing has changed.

"We have detected speed restrictions on Starshield on our Ukrainian territory again, in the temporarily occupied parts of Ukraine," President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Thursday at the European Political Community summit in Ireland. "The speed limit is back at 161 kilometers per hour—that's 100 miles per hour." Without the speed, he said, Ukraine's interceptor drones cannot catch Russia's jet-powered Shahed drones: "This means more Russian strikes get through." SpaceX's media relations department replied: "This is false. There has been no change," Ars Technica reported.

"These are not random errors but systematic speed restrictions," Zelenskyy said, and Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense technology adviser, confirmed that "the speed of Starlink operation during movement was again limited," CNBC reported; SpaceX declined to comment to that outlet. Similar limits imposed last month were lifted by Oct. 24. Elon Musk has said that "Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS," and European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier called the reported limits, "if confirmed, unacceptable," Ars noted.

Xplore cites Starlink in its restructuring

Canadian rural internet provider Xplore won creditor protection from an Ontario court on Nov. 10 after missing interest payments, and the court's endorsement named "the rise of satellite internet alternatives including Space X's Starlink, which is soon to be joined by Amazon Leo" among its problems, alongside fiber-build delays that held back revenue and government funding, Cartt reported.

IV · The Merger Talks

SpaceX–Tesla talks are said to progress

A deal could be announced as soon as December, though it may slip into 2027, according to a new report.

Boards and advisers for SpaceX and Tesla are "hammering out the terms of the tie-up and governance structure of the combined entity," and the companies have discussed an announcement as soon as December or, in another scenario, in early 2027, Bloomberg reported Thursday, citing people familiar with the private discussions. The tie-up has an estimated value of $3.5 trillion, The Verge noted. One complication is how to keep Tesla's business in China separate from SpaceX's government and defense contracts. Neither company has announced a deal.

V · AI & Compute

$74 billion for two supercompute campuses

SpaceX detailed a $24 billion Starbase site and a $50 billion Arizona campus, with water money for Brownsville and lower power bills promised in Cochise County.

SpaceX's two planned supercompute projects, at Starbase in Texas's Rio Grande Valley and in Cochise County, Arizona, "represent more than $74 billion in planned American investment," the company said in an update on Thursday. In the Valley it plans to invest more than $24 billion, on top of more than $15 billion already spent on Starship and related infrastructure at Starbase; the project has generated more than 3,000 construction jobs and is to support more than 500 permanent ones. The facility, near the Port of Brownsville and the city landfill, "will generate its own electricity rather than drawing power from the public grid."

SpaceX also committed $220 million to expand water and wastewater infrastructure for Brownsville and Cameron County, "the largest private contribution to Brownsville's water future in the city's history," and has already given $40 million to the Brownsville Public Utilities Board. The projects are to add about 20 million gallons a day of supply independent of the Rio Grande; the city keeps ownership, and SpaceX "is not acquiring water rights." Brownsville disannexed about 444 acres of the site to the City of Starbase, where SpaceX is cleaning up decades of illegal dumping, including "thousands of illegally dumped tires."

The Arizona campus

In Cochise County, SpaceX is seeking approval for a roughly $50 billion campus expected to create about 5,500 construction jobs and 1,000 permanent jobs, working with local electric cooperatives in a way that could cut members' bills "by up to 20%," and using dry cooling "with no planned industrial groundwater use or new wells," the company said. "SpaceX builds the infrastructure of the future," SpaceX posted at 11:08 a.m. EST, and Elon Musk shared it two minutes later: "SpaceX community benefits."
